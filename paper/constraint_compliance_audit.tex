\begin{abstract}
This is an initial, preliminary audit of constraint-violation rate (CVR) in TabPFN, a genuine
tabular foundation model, evaluated on real relational and tabular benchmarks rather than
synthetic data. We ask whether TabPFN's predictions respect the integrity and domain
constraints that govern the databases it predicts over, using a constraint taxonomy spanning
eight generic database-integrity types (referential integrity, cardinality, aggregate
consistency, temporal order, functional dependency, denial constraint, range/domain,
monotonicity) plus domain-sourced rules requiring external knowledge. Every constraint is
verified against ground truth before being used to audit predictions, across three benchmarks:
rel-f1 (Formula 1 results), rel-salt (SAP enterprise sales data, 8 tasks), and HELOC (consumer
credit risk), and every prediction-level rate is compared with the rate at which the data itself
breaks the same constraint on the same test items. TabPFN was run as a single, untuned
configuration -- on rel-salt with a necessarily small training subsample, evaluated on each
task's full test split, and on rel-f1 via RelArena's TabPFN-Rel relational pipeline -- so the
TabPFN results reported here are preliminary. LightGBM, trained on the full available data with
a fixed seed, is included throughout as a reference point, not as the paper's primary subject.
In this initial pass, TabPFN's predicted shipping points and plants contradicted the
enterprise's dependency 7.5 times as often as the data does on the same items (0.62\% vs.\
0.083\%), and its top-three predictions broke a per-window cardinality bound in 11 of 30
Formula 1 windows where the data breaks it in none; on within-order agreement it was at least as
coherent as the data, reproducing exactly the data's own genuine exceptions, and it was more
monotonic than the LightGBM reference on HELOC. We also find that the LightGBM reference is not
reproducible without a fixed seed: across five runs of identical code and data, its
shipping-point accuracy ranged from 0.28\% to 97.98\%. Finally, a first test of
constraint-conditioned decoding -- conditioning a frozen model's per-cell predictive
distributions on the constraint -- on the LightGBM reference removed every violation, lowered the
joint log-loss by 2.71 nats per item, and, once the probabilities were calibrated, raised the
share of items with both shipping point and plant correct from 89.96\% to 94.61\%, matching but
not beating a simple confidence-based repair. We hold all of these as preliminary observations
pending replication with several seeds and with TabPFN as the conditioned model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A relational or tabular foundation model deployed to autocomplete a missing field, forecast an
outcome, or classify a row is implicitly making a claim about a structured world with its own
invariants: keys are unique, foreign keys resolve, aggregates sum correctly, some fields
determine others, and domain-specific rules hold. Accuracy against a held-out label says
nothing about whether a model's predictions, taken together, respect these invariants.

This report is an initial audit of one such model, TabPFN (a pretrained tabular foundation
model, not a model trained from scratch per task), for constraint-violation rate (CVR) across
real relational and tabular benchmarks. TabPFN is the paper's subject: it is the kind of model
the underlying research question is actually about, an architecture trained once and applied
to new tasks via in-context learning rather than gradient descent on each new dataset.
LightGBM, a conventional gradient-boosted-tree model trained from scratch on each task's own
full training data, is included as a familiar reference point for calibrating what a CVR
number means in absolute terms -- not because the paper is about comparing the two models, and
not on equal footing: LightGBM was trained on full datasets, while TabPFN's CPU inference
limits confined its training to a small subsample on the largest benchmark. Results for the two models
are reported separately throughout, not merged into head-to-head tables, because they are not
run under comparable conditions.

We treat this as a preliminary, single-configuration pass: no hyperparameter tuning, no
repeated seeds, and (on rel-salt) a training sample far smaller than what LightGBM sees. Our
contributions are: (1) a constraint taxonomy spanning generic database-integrity properties and
domain-sourced rules, each with at least one real, empirically-verified instance; (2)
functional-dependency constraints derived from an enterprise database's own Operational
Business Knowledge Graph (OBKG) rather than hand-invented; (3) an initial, preliminary picture
of TabPFN's constraint-violation behavior on real relational data, with every rate set against
the data's own rate on the same test items and LightGBM's behavior reported alongside as
reference context, including one case where a same-relation consistency check alone would have
understated a real failure in an unseeded run of the reference baseline; and (4) a first test of
constraint-conditioned decoding, a training-free way to make a frozen model's predictions
satisfy a constraint by construction.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Constraint taxonomy}

We distinguish two tiers. \textbf{Tier 1 (database integrity)} covers eight generic constraint
types that hold for any well-formed relational database regardless of subject matter:
referential integrity, cardinality, aggregate consistency, temporal order, functional
dependency, denial constraint, range/domain, and monotonicity. \textbf{Tier 2
(domain-sourced)} covers rules whose justification requires external knowledge -- a sport's
rulebook, an ERP system's real business practice, or a fixed regulatory vocabulary -- rather
than following from the schema alone. The distinction is not always sharp (a functional
dependency can itself reflect real-world business knowledge), but it separates constraints
checkable from schema structure alone from those requiring a domain authority. Table
\ref{tab:examples} gives one real, verified example per type, drawn from this audit rather
than invented.

\subsection{Benchmarks}

\textbf{rel-f1}: RelBench's Formula 1 database, 1950--2023, three official tasks
(driver-position, driver-dnf, driver-top3). \textbf{rel-salt}: RelBench v2's SAP SALT-KG
enterprise sales database (2.3M line-item rows, 500K order headers), eight official
autocomplete tasks spanning item-level (item-plant, item-shippoint, item-incoterms) and
header-level (sales-office, sales-group, sales-payterms, sales-shipcond, sales-incoterms)
entities. \textbf{HELOC}: FICO's single-table consumer credit-risk dataset, audited for
monotonicity rather than relational constraints.

\subsection{Models}

\textbf{TabPFN v3 (primary model)}, run as a single untuned configuration in two different
integration paths depending on benchmark, both preliminary:

\begin{itemize}
\item On \textbf{rel-salt}, we used the plain \texttt{tabpfn} client package (v3 backend)
  directly on hand-built flat features, in-process. TabPFN's CPU inference has a documented
  10-class / $\sim$10{,}000-row soft advisory limit and a hard, unbypassable 160-class ceiling
  (confirmed empirically: sales-group's full 502-class vocabulary -- of which a random
  8{,}000-row training draw still contains 378 distinct values -- triggered a
  \texttt{TabPFNValidationError} even with \texttt{ignore\_pretraining\_limits=True}, which we
  set to run past the soft 10-class limit). Because rel-salt's full training tables run to
  1.6M rows, we trained on an 8{,}000-row random subsample per task -- small enough to fit
  TabPFN's practical CPU limits, but far smaller than what a from-scratch model like LightGBM
  can use, and not stratified by class (infeasible across up to 502 classes in an
  8{,}000-row budget). Predictions were then evaluated on each task's full test split (up to
  402{,}855 rows for item-level tasks), batched in groups of 1{,}024 rows to bound memory, so
  only the training side remains a feasibility trial -- the evaluation side is fully powered.
\item On \textbf{rel-f1}, we used RelArena's \texttt{tabpfn-rel-local} pipeline (TabPFN-Rel),
  which automatically synthesizes relational features via multi-hop deep feature synthesis
  (DFS) across the database's foreign-key graph, rather than the hand-built flat features used
  on rel-salt, and performs its own internal tuning (10 trials) over a depth/hyperparameter
  grid. This is a materially different pipeline from the rel-salt integration, included
  because it is RelArena's standard way of running TabPFN on a relational benchmark; the two
  should not be read as the same experimental protocol.
\item On \textbf{HELOC}, we used the same plain \texttt{tabpfn} client package directly on
  HELOC's own tabular features, also on CPU for consistency with rel-salt's device choice
  above. HELOC's single table and $\sim$2{,}000-row monotonicity test set are far below
  TabPFN's practical limits, so CPU is not itself a feasibility constraint here the way it is
  for rel-salt; a GPU rerun gives closely similar but not bit-identical violation rates
  (floating-point operations are not guaranteed identical across CPU and GPU backends even at
  a fixed random seed), which is why we report one device consistently rather than treating
  the two as interchangeable.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{LightGBM (reference baseline)}, trained on the full available training data for every
rel-salt and HELOC task with a fixed, untuned configuration (200 estimators; 63 leaves on
rel-salt, 31 on HELOC), a fixed seed and LightGBM's deterministic mode
(\texttt{random\_state=42}, \texttt{deterministic=True}, \texttt{force\_row\_wise=True}) -- a
conventional, non-foundation-model baseline included so CVR numbers have a familiar point of
reference, not as the object of this study. On rel-f1, LightGBM's predictions come from
RelArena's own LightGBM baseline (its features and its 10-trial tuning), like TabPFN-Rel's.
Earlier unseeded runs of the same configuration were not reproducible (Section
\ref{sec:masking}); every LightGBM number reported here comes from the seeded run, which
reproduces bit for bit on the same machine.

\subsection{Procedure}

Every constraint was first checked against real ground truth -- not assumed from a schema
diagram -- before being used to audit any model's predictions, following the same discipline
for all three benchmarks: compute the violation rate, and if it is unexpectedly non-zero,
either find and fix a real bug or document the non-zero rate as a genuine data property. This
discipline caught two implementation bugs during the audit (a temporal-censoring bug that made
both models degenerate into constant-class predictors, and a $\sim$1000x constraint-engine
performance bug from unprojected self-joins on rel-salt's 2.3M-row table), both fixed before
any result reported here was computed. For every prediction-level check we also report the rate
at which the data itself breaks it on exactly the audited test items, by running the same check
on the true labels (Section \ref{sec:gt-test}): that rate, not zero, is the floor a perfectly
accurate model would reach.

All decision-level violation rates reported in Section 3 are accompanied by a Wilson 95\%
confidence interval rather than a bare point estimate, since several checks -- most visibly
rel-f1's 30-window cardinality check and HELOC's $\sim$2{,}000-row monotonicity checks -- rest
on samples small enough that a point estimate alone would overstate precision. rel-salt's
prediction-level checks run on the full test split (Section 2.3) and carry correspondingly
tight intervals, though its training sample remains small (below).

\paragraph{Scope of this initial pass.} This is a first, preliminary audit, not a finished
benchmark. Concretely: (1) TabPFN was run once per task, with no hyperparameter tuning on
rel-salt and RelArena's own fixed 10-trial tuning on rel-f1 -- neither is a tuning sweep
matched between the two models; (2) rel-salt's TabPFN training subsample (8{,}000 rows per
task) was chosen for CPU feasibility, not statistical power, even though prediction is now
evaluated on each task's full test split -- the Wilson intervals reported alongside each
decision-level rate reflect whatever sample actually underlies that check, not a uniformly
small one; (3) each configuration was run once, not repeated across random seeds,
so we cannot yet distinguish a stable pattern from sampling noise; (4) LightGBM's numbers are
reported as a reference point throughout, never merged into a single head-to-head statistic
with TabPFN's, because the two were not run under comparable data-scale conditions. We draw
only preliminary conclusions from these results (Section \ref{sec:discussion}) and treat every
TabPFN number as provisional pending a matched-scale, repeated-seed follow-up.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{TabPFN's preliminary CVR pattern}

In this initial, single-configuration pass, TabPFN's rel-salt predictions showed low
constraint-violation rates on the full test split (at or below 0.62\%) alongside
high accuracy ($\geq$97.0\% on every runnable task); on item-shippoint it was more accurate
than the seeded LightGBM reference (97.02\% vs.\ 90.02\%) despite training on 8{,}000 rows
against 1.6 million. Low is not the same as coherent, however. Against the data on the same
items (Table \ref{tab:gt-test}), TabPFN breaks the cross-task shipping point $\to$ plant
dependency 7.5 times as often as the data does, while on within-order plant agreement it is
more coherent than the data and never predicts its genuine exceptions. The right target for a
constraint-violation rate is the data's own rate, not zero: a model can err in both directions.
TabPFN was also more monotonic than the LightGBM reference on both HELOC features (10.53\%/19.69\%
vs.\ 17.84\%/32.19\%). We read these as encouraging preliminary signals, not a settled finding: the
rel-salt training sample is small (8{,}000 rows per task), untuned, and run once, so we cannot
yet separate a real property of TabPFN's predictions from sampling noise in which 8{,}000
rows it happened to see -- though the evaluation side is no longer a small-sample concern,
now that it runs on the full test split. The one number that
stands out enough to flag for follow-up is rel-f1's top3\_predictions\_per\_window via
TabPFN-Rel (36.67\%, 11 of 30 windows) -- notably higher than the LightGBM reference's figure
on the same check (13.33\%, 4 of 30), though with only 30 test windows a handful of counts
move the percentage a great deal: a two-sided Fisher exact test on the 11-vs-4-of-30 counts
gives $p=0.07$, so at this sample size the gap does not reach significance. The data itself
respects the bound in all 30 windows (Table \ref{tab:gt-test}), so every violating window is a
genuine error. TabPFN-Rel's RelArena pipeline is also a different feature-engineering path from
anything used on rel-salt (Section 2.3). We therefore read this result as evidence that per-cell
predictors generally lack a mechanism for enforcing this kind of cross-row cardinality
constraint, not as a ranking between the two models audited here, and not as evidence about
TabPFN specifically.

\subsection{An unseeded reference run, and why one constraint is not enough}
\label{sec:masking}

An earlier, unseeded run of the LightGBM reference illustrates why this audit uses more than one
constraint type per relation. In that run, LightGBM's within-order consistency check reported
0.00\% violations for its item-shippoint predictions -- an apparently perfect result -- while its
item-shippoint accuracy was 2.97\%, below the task's 48.2\% majority-class baseline: the
predictions had collapsed onto a handful of classes, so items in the same order trivially
agreed with each other on a wrong answer. The cross-task functional-dependency check
(shippingpoint\_pred\_determines\_plant\_pred, derived from the same OBKG relation used to verify
the ground-truth \texttt{SHIPPINGPOINT}$\to$\texttt{PLANT} dependency) exposed the same failure
directly, at 27.29\%, because a model whose shipping-point predictions are near-random cannot
also produce a plant prediction consistent with them.

That collapse, however, was a property of that run, not of LightGBM on this task. The
configuration set no random seed, and LightGBM's multithreaded tree building is not
reproducible without one. Across five runs of identical code on identical data -- two unseeded
runs on two machines, two runs with seed 0 on two machines, and one with seed 42 --
item-shippoint accuracy was 2.97\%, 59.67\%, 97.98\%, 0.28\% and 90.02\%; sales-shipcond accuracy
ranged from 11.46\% to 86.16\% and sales-group accuracy from 1.25\% to 86.67\%, while the other
five tasks agreed to within 0.03 points. A sweep with the seed as the only setting changed
showed seed 0 to be an unlucky draw for sales-group (0.72\%, against 85.5--85.8\% for seeds 1,
2, 42 and 123). With a fixed seed and LightGBM's deterministic mode, two runs on the same
machine gave identical results (on one machine we checked every prediction), though not across
machines (seed 0 gave 97.98\% on one machine and 0.28\% on another). Every LightGBM number in this report comes from the seed-42 run, in which
item-shippoint does not collapse (90.02\%) and the same-relation and cross-task checks agree that
its predictions are incoherent (0.60\% and 6.70\%).

We take three things from this. First, methodologically: a single same-relation consistency
check is not sufficient evidence of model competence, for either model audited here -- a
collapsed predictor satisfies it trivially, and only an independently sourced cross-task
constraint (or accuracy) exposes the failure. Second, a reference baseline must be run with a
fixed seed and checked for reproducibility before its numbers are compared with anything; ours
initially was not, and its weakest numbers turned out to be seed artifacts. Third, the problem
surfaced only because two runs of the same reference were compared; a single run would have
been reported as a stable property of LightGBM.

\subsection{Constraint engineering at real scale}

A naive self-join implementation of the within-order and functional-dependency checks took
15--65 minutes per constraint on rel-salt's 2.3M-row item table, driven by nullable-dtype
columns carried unnecessarily through the join. Projecting each join down to only the columns
the predicate needs reduced this to under a second per constraint with identical violation
counts. Constraint auditing at production scale is not just a modeling exercise; the
constraint engine itself must be engineered for the data volumes real relational benchmarks
involve.

\section{A first test of constraint-conditioned decoding}
\label{sec:ccd}

The audit shows per-cell predictors breaking constraints that hold in the data. A remedy that
needs no retraining is to condition the model's per-cell predictive distributions on the
constraint: take the product of the distributions of the cells a constraint couples, restrict it
to the assignments that satisfy the constraint, renormalize, and decode the most probable
satisfying assignment (hard conditioning); or, for constraints with exceptions, down-weight
violating assignments by a strength $\delta$ fitted on held-out data (soft conditioning). For the
shipping point $\to$ plant dependency, an item's shipping point $s$ and plant $p$ must satisfy
$p = g(s)$. The model puts mass $q(C) = \sum_s q_{\mathrm{ship}}(s)\, q_{\mathrm{plant}}(g(s))$ on
satisfying pairs, its coherence deficit is $\Delta = -\log q(C)$, and the conditioned decision is
$\arg\max_s q_{\mathrm{ship}}(s)\, q_{\mathrm{plant}}(g(s))$. At order level, every item of an order
must in addition share one plant. All formulas are exact and are checked against brute-force
enumeration in our code.

We tested this on the seeded LightGBM reference on rel-salt, on all 402{,}855 test items in
88{,}942 orders. The map $g$ is each shipping point's majority plant among the training items,
not SAP's configuration table, which is not part of rel-salt. Under this $g$ the data itself
breaks the dependency for 1.16\% of test items: 4{,}319 items (1.07\%) have a shipping point
never seen in training, which no decoder can recover, and 338 (0.084\%) are genuine exceptions.
We compare raw per-cell decisions with a greedy repair that changes the less confident of the two
cells, a Hamming-nearest repair that ignores confidence (it keeps the shipping point and sets its
plant), and hard and soft conditioning, each on the model's own probabilities and after per-task
temperature scaling. Every fitted parameter (temperatures and $\delta$) is cross-fitted: the test
orders are split into two halves, and each half is decoded with parameters fitted on the other.
Intervals are 95\% bootstrap intervals that resample whole orders.

\paragraph{Conditioning only helps once the probabilities are calibrated.} On LightGBM's own
probabilities, conditioning removes every violation but barely improves decisions (+0.36 points
of items with both cells correct) and lowers plant accuracy by 7.4 points: it turns 29{,}870
correct plants wrong and corrects 124. LightGBM is so overconfident on rel-salt -- its
second-ranked shipping point typically gets a probability around $10^{-71}$, and its own joint
log-loss is about 8{,}900 nats per item -- that on a contradicting item the probabilities cannot
tell which cell is wrong. After per-task temperature scaling, conditioning raises the share of
items with both cells correct from 89.96\% to 94.61\% (+4.65 points, [4.38, 4.92]), keeps plant
accuracy (99.08\% vs.\ 99.09\%), and raises the share of orders with every item correct from
79.28\% to 86.22\% (+6.94 points, [6.78, 7.11]). The best single temperature for the
shipping-point model lies at our search limit ($10^7$): it flattens those probabilities to almost
uniform and keeps only their ranking, so the gain comes from trusting the well-calibrated plant
model (temperature $\approx 1.5$) and taking the best-ranked shipping point within its plant.

\paragraph{On decisions, conditioning matches but does not beat a confidence-based repair.}
Greedy repair reaches 94.60\% against 94.61\% for conditioning; conditioning is never worse on any
item and better on 32 of 402{,}855 (+0.008 points, [0.004, 0.013]), which is detectable but
negligible. Repair that ignores confidence gains essentially nothing (14 items). The decision
gain therefore comes from using calibrated confidence to decide which cell to change, which both
methods do.

\paragraph{Conditioning improves the probabilities, which repair cannot.} On items whose true
labels satisfy the dependency, hard conditioning lowers the joint log-loss by exactly each item's
coherence deficit, 2.71 nats per item on average ([2.69, 2.73]), as the theory predicts. Soft
conditioning, with $\delta$ fitted on held-out orders, stays finite on the 338 genuine exceptions,
where hard conditioning assigns zero probability, and lowers the log-loss over all scored items
from 4.42 to 1.72 nats. Conditioning whole orders adds coherence beyond conditioning items one at
a time on the model's own probabilities (item-level conditioning leaves 969 orders whose items
disagree on the plant, order-level conditioning none), but once the probabilities are calibrated,
item-level conditioning already leaves only 12 such orders.

These are single-seed results for one model, one constraint and one database; the intervals
cover which items happen to be in the test set, not training randomness, which Section
\ref{sec:masking} shows can be large for this reference. Whether conditioning beats repair on
decisions, and whether TabPFN's probabilities need calibrating first, remain open until the same
experiment is run with TabPFN.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Unequal training scale (evaluation scale is now matched).} TabPFN's rel-salt
  results use an 8{,}000-row training subsample against LightGBM's full $\sim$1.6M training
  rows, so the two models' predictions are not fit under comparable conditions. Both are now
  evaluated on the same full test split (up to 402{,}855 rows per task), which removes the
  evaluation-side power gap the original 1{,}000-row pilot had, but the training-side gap
  remains and a model trained on 8{,}000 rows is not a matched comparison to one trained on
  1.6M regardless of how large the test set is. On item-level tasks, TabPFN's feature set also
  drops five near-unique identifier columns that LightGBM retains (Section 2.3).
\item \textbf{Single run, no matched tuning.} TabPFN was run once per task, untuned on
  rel-salt, and under RelArena's own fixed 10-trial tuning on rel-f1 -- a different,
  non-matched regime. We cannot yet distinguish a stable pattern from a lucky or unlucky draw.
\item \textbf{A hard architectural ceiling.} TabPFN has an unbypassable 160-class limit that
  excludes it entirely from sales-group (502 classes in the full vocabulary, still 378 within
  just its 8{,}000-row training draw); every comparison involving that task is one-sided.
\item \textbf{A reference baseline that is sensitive to its seed.} Without a fixed seed,
  LightGBM's accuracy on item-shippoint, sales-shipcond and sales-group varied enormously
  between runs of identical code on identical data (Section \ref{sec:masking}); with a fixed
  seed it reproduces on one machine, but not across machines. We report one seed (42); other
  seeds can give materially different numbers on those three tasks. This is a property of the
  reference baseline, not of TabPFN.
\item \textbf{Two different TabPFN pipelines.} rel-f1's TabPFN-Rel (RelArena, DFS relational
  features, internal tuning) and rel-salt's plain TabPFN (hand-built flat features, no
  tuning) are not the same experimental setup; results from the two should not be pooled or
  treated as evidence about a single ``TabPFN'' condition.
\item \textbf{Small denominators.} rel-f1's top3\_predictions\_per\_window check has only 30
  test windows; an 11-vs-4 count difference reads as a large percentage gap (36.7\% vs.\
  13.3\%).
\item \textbf{Conditioning tested on one model and one constraint.} Section \ref{sec:ccd} uses
  only the LightGBM reference, one seed, and a shipping point $\to$ plant map learned from
  training items rather than SAP's configuration table. Its decision gain depends on
  calibrating the probabilities first, and the best temperature for the shipping-point model lay
  at our search limit.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion (preliminary)}

This is an initial, single-configuration audit, not a finished evaluation. In this first pass,
TabPFN showed low constraint-violation rates and high accuracy across most of rel-salt's
tasks, but against the data's own rates on the same items it breaks the cross-task shipping
point $\to$ plant dependency 7.5 times as often as the data does, and the rel-f1 top-three bound
in 11 of 30 windows where the data breaks it in none; on within-order plant agreement it is more
coherent than the data. It was also more monotonic than the LightGBM reference on HELOC. We hold
all of these as preliminary observations: TabPFN's rel-salt runs used a small, untuned,
single-seed training sample (evaluation runs on the full test split), and its rel-f1 numbers
come from a different pipeline (TabPFN-Rel) than its rel-salt numbers, so the two should not be
pooled into one claim about ``TabPFN.''

Three methodological lessons are more durable than any single number. A constraint-violation
rate must be compared with the data's own rate on the same items, not with zero, because a model
can be less coherent than the data or more. A same-relation consistency check can certify a
collapsed predictor as perfectly coherent, which only an independently sourced cross-task
constraint exposes. And a reference baseline must be seeded and checked for reproducibility:
our unseeded LightGBM reference ranged from 0.28\% to 97.98\% accuracy on one task across runs
of identical code. A first test of constraint-conditioned decoding on that reference removed
every violation and lowered the joint log-loss by exactly the coherence deficit; with calibrated
probabilities it also raised the share of fully correct items by 4.65 points, matching but not
beating a simple confidence-based repair. A repeated-seed follow-up, with TabPFN as the
conditioned model, is the natural next step.
